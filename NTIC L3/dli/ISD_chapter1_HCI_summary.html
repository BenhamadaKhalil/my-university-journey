Interactive Software & Human-Computer Interaction

Your clean exam-ready summary 🎯 — with examples, visuals & key takeaways

🖥️ What is an interactive system?
A system that allows two-way communication between user and computer. The user acts → the system responds → the user reacts again. This back-and-forth loop is what makes a system interactive.
💡 Everyday examples
Smartphones 📱 · Video games 🎮 · Websites 🌐 · Word processors 📝 · Mobile apps
📊 Levels of interaction
🔇
No interaction
Non-interactive
e.g. Compiler — you give input, it processes silently, gives output. No dialogue.
🚦
Limited interaction
Mostly automatic
e.g. Traffic lights — follow a fixed timer, no user control needed.
🎮
Fully interactive
User in control
e.g. Games, apps — every click/tap produces an immediate response.
🧩 HCI — key terminology
🔬 Human-Computer Interaction (HCI)
The broader concept — describes the process, experience, and quality of how humans and machines work together. Includes behaviors, workflows, feedback loops, and user experience. This is our course focus.
🖱️ Human-Computer Interface
The physical or digital components through which humans and machines communicate — touchscreens, keyboards, buttons, dashboards, graphical UIs.
⚠️ HCI = Human-Computer Interaction OR Human-Computer Interface — same acronym, two meanings! In French: IHM = Interaction(s) Homme-Machine / Interface(s) Homme-Machine
🧠 Key insight
When you use a computer, you're not really interacting with the computer — you're interacting with your task, through the computer. The computer is just the mediator.

✍️ When writing an essay, are you thinking about the keyboard? No — you're thinking about your words.
📅 Historical timeline of HCI
1940s
Computers appear — huge, expensive, locked in special rooms. Only trained engineers could use them (wires, confusing commands like grep). No one cared about ease of use.
1940–1980
Computers existed but weren't user-friendly. Specialists only. Normal people had zero access.
1980s 🚀
Everything changes. Computers move from locked labs into offices and homes. HCI emerges to make them accessible to everyone.
1983 ⭐ Birth of HCI
Three major events:
ACM SIGCHI first conference Book on computer psychology published Apple Macintosh announced 🍎
🧑‍🤝‍🧑 Why HCI is challenging — human variability
💻 Computers are simple. Humans are complex.
Computers follow exact instructions and behave the same every time. Humans are varied, unpredictable, and ever-changing — making design much harder.
👶👴
Age: A 7-year-old uses tech very differently from a 70-year-old senior.
♿
Physical abilities: Some are colorblind, visually impaired, use wheelchairs, or have limited hand movement.
🧑‍💻
Experience: A tech expert navigates easily; a beginner gets lost on the same interface.
🌍
Language & culture: Reading direction varies (left-to-right vs. right-to-left like Arabic). Expectations differ culturally.
😴
Temporary state: A tired or stressed user interacts very differently than an alert one.
🌍 Real-world examples of variability impact
🖱️ A button easy to click with a mouse → impossible for someone with trembling hands
🔤 Normal-size text → too small for someone with poor vision
🇬🇧 An English app → useless for Arabic-only speakers
⚡ A reflex-based game → fun for young players, frustrating for seniors
❓ The big questions HCI asks
🤔 Why do people make mistakes when using technology?
🧠 Why do people forget features they've used before?
😤 Why is installing software so frustrating?
📱 Why is driving while on the phone so dangerous?
📲 Why are people so drawn to social media?
🎓 Two sides of HCI
📚 Academic side
Studying & researching how people interact with technology. About gaining knowledge through observation and research.
🎨 Design side
Applying research to create better systems. Focused on UX design and UI design — turning knowledge into action.
🔑 Core principle: "Know the user"
You can't design one system that works perfectly for everyone — that's impossible.
Instead: identify your target users · study their behavior · test with real users · adjust based on feedback.

"The more we understand humans, the better our chances of designing systems that work as intended. Our work is never precise — it is always approximate."
