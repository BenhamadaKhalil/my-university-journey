# **Chapter 2: Copyright and Neighbouring Rights**

## **1. Introduction:**
- **Intellectual Property** consists of legal rights related to intellectual creations. It is divided into:
  - **Industrial Property** (e.g., patents, trademarks).
  - **Literary and Artistic Property** (e.g., copyright and neighbouring rights).

## **2. Concept of Author:**
- The author of a literary or artistic work, under Algerian law (Art. 13 - Order 03/05, 2003), is the **natural person** who created it.
- A **legal entity** may also be considered an author.
- The **copyright holder** is presumed to be the person or legal entity in whose name the work was declared or made publicly available, unless proven otherwise.
- If the work is published without the author’s name, the person who made it publicly available is presumed to represent the rights holder.

## **3. Concept of an Author’s Work:**
- An **author’s work** is defined as any creation of the mind, literary or artistic, that has **individual character**.
- **Examples of an Author’s Work**:
  - **Musical Works**: Operas, folk songs, symphonies, melodies, and lyrics.
  - **Literary Works**: Novels, short stories, scripts, poems, plays, historical narratives, biographies, newspaper articles.
  - **Artistic Works**: Sculptures, theater, paintings, drawings, prints, photographs, architectural works (models, plans).
  - **Choreographic Works**: Ballets, mimes.
  - **Audiovisual Works**: Films, video games, TV programs, cartoons.
  - **Publicity Creations**.
  - **Digital Works**: Computer programs, software, databases.

## **4. Copyright:**
- **Copyright** grants exclusive rights to the author once a work is created, automatically protected by the **Intellectual Property Code (CPI)**.
- Copyright works are often marked with the symbol **©**.
- The author has two types of rights over the work:
  - **Moral Rights**.
  - **Economic Rights** (financial rights).

### **Moral Rights:**
- **Moral Rights** are **inalienable** (cannot be sold) and **imprescriptible** (eternal), and cannot be waived.
- These rights represent the **personal bond** between the author and the work.

### **Key Moral Rights:**
1. **Right of Paternity**: The author has the right to claim authorship of the work or remain anonymous.
2. **Right of Disclosure**: The author decides when and how the work is made public.
3. **Right to Respect for the Work**: The author can object to any modification or alteration of their work.
4. **Right of Withdrawal and Repentance**: The author can withdraw the work or make changes at any time, without needing to justify the decision.

### **Economic Rights:**
- **Economic Rights** are related to the **financial exploitation** of the work. These rights can be **transferred**, unlike **moral rights**, which are perpetual.
- **Examples of Economic Rights**:
  1. **Reproduction Right**: The right to prevent unauthorized copying of the work.
  2. **Public Performance**: The author can decide if the work is publicly exhibited or performed.
  3. **Adaptation and Translation Rights**: The author controls the translation, adaptation, and distribution of their work.

### **Transfer of Copyright:**
- The author may choose to transfer **economic rights** to others, either for **remuneration** or **free of charge**.
- The author may also transfer only part of their economic rights (e.g., translation rights for international markets).

### **Duration of Copyright:**
- The duration of **copyright protection** varies by country:
  - In countries that are members of the **Berne Convention**, copyright lasts for the **author’s lifetime** plus **50 years** after death.
  - If there are multiple authors, the protection duration is based on the **last surviving co-author**.
  - For **anonymous works** or works published under **pseudonyms**, copyright lasts **50 years** from the publication date.
  - After the copyright term expires, the work enters the **public domain**, and anyone can reproduce it freely.
  - **Moral rights** last indefinitely, even after the economic rights expire.

---

## **5. Neighbouring Rights:**
- **Neighbouring rights** (also known as related rights) are granted to those who play a role in making the work accessible to the public.
- These rights do not apply to the creator of the work, but rather to those who **mediate** between the creator and the public, such as:
  - **Performing Artists**: (e.g., actors, singers, musicians).
  - **Producers of Sound Recordings**: (e.g., recordings on CDs or cassettes).
  - **Broadcasting Organizations**: (e.g., TV and radio stations).
  
### **Duration of Neighbouring Rights:**
- Neighbouring rights last for **50 years** from the **date of performance**, **recording**, or **broadcast**.
- These rights ensure that intermediaries are compensated for their work in making the creator’s work publicly accessible.

---

## **Key Takeaways:**
- **Copyright** provides creators with exclusive control over their works, granting **moral rights** and **economic rights**.
- **Moral rights** protect the personal connection between the author and their work, while **economic rights** allow authors to financially exploit their works.
- **Neighbouring rights** protect those who contribute to the public’s access to the work, such as performers and producers.
- The **duration** of **copyright protection** typically lasts for the author’s lifetime plus 50 years, after which the work becomes the **public domain**.
